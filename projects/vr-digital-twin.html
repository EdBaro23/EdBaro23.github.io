Professional project

Virtual Reality Digital Twin

A walkthrough digital twin of a working warehouse: reconstructed from laser scans, replayed from recorded location data, and inspected from inside a VR headset.

Type
Professional, digital division of a logistics group
Role
Sole developer
Target hardware
HTC Vive and other SteamVR headsets
Outcome
Presented to the CEO and CDO; pilot trials scoped

The problem

How much a warehouse can move in a day is limited by how it is arranged. When several vehicles need the same aisle at the same time, queues form. That congestion accumulates in thirty-second increments, so it lands in the monthly totals without ever being visible on the floor.

What I built

VRDT reconstructs a real warehouse in 3D from laser scans and photography, then replays the recorded movement of its vehicles and goods through it. A manager puts on a headset, stands inside a copy of their own warehouse and watches a shift back: pausing, slowing it down, walking into the aisles where the queues form.

Key capabilities

  • Twin generation from location data, photography and laser scans.
  • Playback control: play, pause, fast-forward and slow down, so a quiet hour can be skimmed and a bad minute crawled through.
  • Grab-to-zoom: squeeze the controller and pull to scale the warehouse, between standing in an aisle and looking down on the whole floor plan.
  • Teleport-based locomotion, chosen over smooth movement to reduce motion sickness in users who are not regular VR users.
  • Object inspection: placing your hand on a vehicle or a pallet reveals its details.

How it was built

I consulted logistics professionals first, so the tool was designed around what a warehouse manager needs rather than what was easiest to render. The application is C# in Unity. Python handles the location data: creating, editing and reshaping it for testing. The environment comes from the company's existing 3D scan pipeline, which I connected to with the colleagues who owned it.

Engineering challenges

Location data is sampled, movement is continuous. Positions are recorded at discrete intervals, so replaying them literally makes every vehicle teleport between samples. Interpolating between the recorded points turns them into continuous motion, and makes playback scrubbable to any moment rather than only to a sample.

No real location data existed while I was building. The sensor pipeline that would feed VRDT did not exist yet, so I wrote a Python tool to generate and edit synthetic data. It also turned out to be better for testing: I could author a specific scenario (two vehicles converging on one aisle, a dropped sample, an implausibly fast movement) and reproduce it on demand.

Where it got to

I presented VRDT to the digital division once the prototype worked, then to the Chief Executive Officer and Chief Digital Officer, who asked me to prepare pilot trials in two client warehouses.

Next steps were a pipeline with the company's Sensors team for collecting real location data, the pilot run against that data, and then full integration with the warehouses.
